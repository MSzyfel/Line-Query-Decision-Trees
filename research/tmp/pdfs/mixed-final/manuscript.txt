                     Planar Line-Query Decision Trees:
                 Approximation and Strong NP-Completeness


                                             October 13, 2026


                                                    Abstract
          A hidden point must be identified from a finite planar hypothesis set using only a supplied
      family of line queries. We minimize either the maximum or the expected number of queries.
      We give polynomial-time constant approximations: a factor below 4.464966 for deterministic
      maximum depth and a factor below 5.182976 for expected depth under arbitrary strictly pos-
      itive priors. For every fixed ε > 0, we give quasipolynomial-time (2 + ε) approximations for
      both objectives. Multigrade scheduling of adjacent support blocks first gives additive bounds
      with leading coefficient arbitrarily close to two; exact logarithmic-depth lookahead removes their
      count and entropy penalties. The geometric step normalizes half-planes around a common wit-
      ness and reduces their convex-hull tests to a circular-interval system. For expected depth, the
      algorithm learns the complete hull-test pattern before recursing. Its first positive answer is paid
      for by queries removed from an optimal tree after restriction; the remaining pattern information
      is paid for by entropy. An exact interval scheduler exponential only in the number of query slots,
      combined with a logarithmic cap and a priority fallback, gives the polynomial maximum-depth
      improvement. Scheduling adjacent supports as blocks credits endpoint information already
      learned during discovery; rational choices between stopping and querying both endpoints, to-
      gether with modified initial coverage batches, sharpen the polynomial expected-depth factor.
      Both quantities telescope over the recursive partitions. Allowing an on-line third answer in-
      creases the approximation factors by at most two. Finally, direct reductions from 3-Partition
      establish strong NP-completeness of both objectives, even for integer-coordinate points on a
      parabola. An exact cloud construction transfers the expected-depth reduction to uniform pri-
      ors. Every query in the algorithms and reductions is a supplied line query. With a distinguished
      all-miss hypothesis and bounded cap size, maximum depth is exactly solvable in polynomial
      time. Weighted singleton/pair path-and-cycle instances admit an EPTAS for expected depth.
      For uniform priors, fixed-size caps in convex position with a distinguished all-miss hypothesis
      admit a PTAS without singleton tests.


1    Introduction
An unknown point is promised to belong to a finite set of planar hypotheses. A query asks on which
side of a specified line the point lies. The line family is part of the input: a strategy may choose the
next query after seeing previous answers, but it may not invent a separator that was not supplied.
Such a strategy is a decision tree; each leaf names the hypothesis identified by the answers on its
path. Every query has unit cost.
    We study two objectives for these trees. The maximum depth is the largest number of queries
used on any hypothesis. The expected depth is the average under a given prior. These objectives
value the same tree differently: maximum depth is governed by its hardest branch, whereas expected
depth can spend more queries on low-probability hypotheses. Our expected-depth guarantees allow
arbitrary strictly positive probabilities.

    The supplied-query restriction is what makes the problem interesting. If arbitrary lines were
allowed, a projection in a direction giving distinct projected coordinates would order the points, and
repeated median splits would give height ⌈log2 n⌉. A supplied family need not contain any balanced
split. For example, if the only useful tests isolate individual points, the worst-case strategy may
have to test them one at a time and use depth n − 1. Thus the information bound log2 n is not
by itself an algorithm: the challenge is to find a good tree using the separators that are actually
available.
    Figure 1 makes the input and output concrete. The left panel has seven supplied lines with varied
orientations and twenty hypotheses placed in distinct cells of their arrangement. The right panel
is one legal decision tree for that instance: successive line-side answers isolate a single hypothesis.
Each internal node uses a line drawn on the left, and its leaves identify the same hypotheses marked
by the numbered points.

1.1     Results and the main ideas
We give polynomial-time constant-factor approximations for both objectives:

The expected-depth bound applies to arbitrary strictly positive priors. We first prove the individual-
vertex factor below 5.445944, then improve it by applying the same polynomial coverage scheduler
to adjacent support blocks. Joint discovery and endpoint accounting preserves the credit from a
negative first endpoint. A coupled choice of block orientations and geometric shift retains this
saving through the first two batches’ coverage guarantees. Selectively querying both endpoints
after a positive answer also reveals pattern information; a rational entropy lower bound credits
it. Changing the initial two coverage budgets and sometimes merging the first two geometric
batches gives the displayed expected-depth factor. A finite rational certificate accounts for both
delayed positive discoveries and the entire zero route. For maximum depth, an exact rank-subset

    The geometric structure behind the algorithms is easiest to see in a state (the current set of
hypotheses consistent with all answers so far) where no line splits much of the remaining mass.
Every test then has a majority answer. The opposite, minority half-planes are small, and Helly’s
theorem gives a point lying on all majority sides. After translating this common witness to the
origin, each query is represented by a point in a normalized plane. The vertices of their convex hull
(the smallest convex set containing those points) suffice to describe the relevant caps: each original
cap is covered by at most two adjacent vertex caps, and the vertex answers of any one hypothesis
form a circular interval : a consecutive block in the cyclic order in which the hull vertices appear
around the polygon, with wrap-around allowed. This turns the geometric part of the problem into
scheduling and searching over circular intervals.

Maximum depth: schedule discovery against child heights. In a flat state, the algorithm
first builds the recursive codes for the hull-pattern classes. It then searches for a positive hull
answer using a schedule whose deadlines are derived from those codes’ actual heights. An optimal-
tree path through the common witness supplies a comparison schedule: classes that remain negative
on a long prefix have already saved that many queries when the optimal tree is restricted to the class.
Each original query has at most two adjacent hull supports, so the comparison schedule consists
of singletons and adjacent pairs rather than arbitrary subsets. Enumerating its first pair turns the
remaining circular intervals into ordinary intervals. A priority-greedy fallback preserves the resulting
credit simultaneously for every deadline. A second, exact scheduler stores subsets of available ranks
in a spatial Cartesian tree. It is polynomial under a logarithmic total cap; simultaneous recursive
bounds for small and large optima make that cap sufficient for the stronger general factor. Endpoint
searches are charged separately, with a smaller allowance when the discovery path already supplies
a negative neighbor. This is how the analysis improves the constant without adding artificial height
to a child code.

Expected depth: pay once for learning a pattern. For expected depth, the algorithm again
discovers the complete hull-answer pattern before recursing. The analysis separates the work into


                                                   3
two parts. First, a class’s constant prefix on an optimal witness path disappears when the optimal
tree is restricted to that class; the saved queries pay for finding the class’s first positive hull answer.
Second, the remaining endpoint information is paid for by the entropy of the pattern that was
not already revealed during discovery. Exact weighted coverage at each integer budget, together
with a deterministic choice among shifted geometric batches, gives a sharper first-cover bound.
Adaptive block modes also credit the information from a second endpoint answer, measured against
a coarsened discovery transcript whose cells retain the individual cap bound. A mass cap on each
adjacent support block forces some targets into later blocks, where the discrete bound is better; the
waiting time of the all-zero pattern covers the complementary case. Both the optimal-depth savings
and the entropy decrease telescope over the recursive partition, so no part of the optimal cost is
charged repeatedly.

Strong hardness: encode bin loads by matching choices. The reductions start from 3-
Partition. Singleton and adjacent-pair queries on disjoint odd paths make each item choose one of
the path’s even-position vertices. A distinguished hypothesis forces every optimal tree to make a
maximum matching on these paths, so an unmatched position represents a bin choice. For maximum
depth, clouds of points turn the resulting bin loads into counts of branches with specified remaining
heights. For expected depth, carefully chosen weights turn them into a quadratic penalty for unequal
loads. The parabola construction realizes every query by an explicit line. Replacing weight w by a
cloud of w uniform points with private singleton tests adds the same internal identification cost to
every matching invoice; this exact identity preserves the reduction.

2     Preliminaries and basic accounting
We collect the model, coding and information inequalities, and geometric terminology used by the
algorithms. Reduction-specific graph and complexity definitions are given in Section 16. For a
positive integer r, write [r] = {1, . . . , r}. The notation |S| denotes set cardinality, while |Q| denotes
the length of a word, vector, or sequence when Q is such an object. The indicator 1[P ] is one if
statement P holds and zero otherwise. We also write 1A (x) = 1[x ∈ A] for the indicator function
of a set A. The floor ⌊x⌋ and ceiling ⌈x⌉ are the greatest integer at most x and the least integer at
least x, respectively. For planar vectors, x · y = x1 y1 + x2 y2 is the dot product.

2.1    Line-query model and objectives
Let H ⊂ R2 be n ≥ 2 distinct points. A finite family L of m supplied lines separates every pair
of hypotheses, meaning that every two distinct points receive different answers to at least one line.


                                                    4
Write a line as the zero set of an affine function fℓ (x) = aℓ · x + bℓ , where aℓ ∈ R2 \ {0} and bℓ ∈ R.
Querying ℓ returns the sign of fℓ (h) for the hidden point h. Every query contributes one to the
depth. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, no hypothesis lies on a supplied line, so the answer is
binary. Section 15 treats a third, on-line outcome.
   After any sequence of queries and answers, the current state is the set of hypotheses whose signs
on all queried lines agree with the answers received. For a state S and a supplied line ℓ, write

                      Sℓ− = {h ∈ S : fℓ (h) < 0},       Sℓ+ = {h ∈ S : fℓ (h) > 0}.

These are the two answer classes; the line splits S when both classes are nonempty.

Definition 2.1 (Line-query decision trees). A binary line-query decision tree is a finite rooted tree
whose internal nodes are labeled by supplied lines and whose two outgoing edges are labeled by
their negative and positive answers. A hypothesis follows the edge matching its answer at each
query. An identifying tree for S has hypothesis labels at its leaves and sends every h ∈ S to a leaf
labeled h. The root has depth zero; a leaf’s depth is the number of queries on its root-to-leaf path,
and the tree’s height is its largest leaf depth.
    A partial decision tree, or stage, may instead stop at leaves labeled by nonempty subsets of S.
Each leaf is labeled by exactly the hypotheses compatible with its path, so these output classes
partition S. Attaching an identifying tree for each class produces a complete identifying tree.

    We suppress a query whenever its answer is already determined by the current candidate set.
In particular, a line is never queried twice on one path. The resulting tree is proper : both children
of every internal node contain hypotheses. A proper binary tree on n hypotheses has n − 1 internal
nodes and depth at most n−1: if I is its number of internal nodes, counting edges gives 2I = I +n−1,
so I = n − 1 and every path has at most that many queries. This convention does not restrict the
optimum: a query with only one nonempty outcome can be deleted, and repeated queries have
already known answers.
    A transcript records the queries and answers observed along a path. In a fixed binary tree the
previous answers determine the next query, so the transcript can also be represented by its answer
word, encoding negative as 0 and positive as 1. A cached answer is an answer already learned
on that path; reusing it or inferring a constant answer costs no additional query. For nonempty
A ⊆ S, the restriction T |A is obtained by deleting leaves outside A, deleting empty branches, and
contracting queries with only one surviving outcome. It is a valid tree on A, and no surviving
hypothesis’s depth increases.

Definition 2.2 (Membership answers and spines). A membership interpretation designates one
answer class A of a supplied binary query. Its answer is a hit when h ∈ A and a miss otherwise. A
hit may be either affine sign; interpreting the answer changes neither the supplied line nor its cost.
    The spine of a distinguished hypothesis is its root-to-leaf path. Another hypothesis exits that
path at its first different answer; the attached subtree is its local exit code. If this occurs at query
position j, a local depth d contributes total depth j +d. If every designated membership set excludes
the distinguished hypothesis, its spine is an all-miss route and exits occur at first hits. A point p
on no supplied line similarly determines a witness path, even when p ∈    / H.

Definition 2.3 (Balanced and flat states). Fix 0 < δ < 1/3. A nonsingleton state S is count-
balanced if some supplied line has at least δ|S| hypotheses on each side. Otherwise it is count-flat.
In the flat case, every line has a unique majority answer, on the side containing more than half of
S. We call this its frozen answer and write Cℓ for the open half-plane on the opposite side. Its
induced answer class Cℓ ∩ S is the minority cap, of size less than δ|S|. We also use “cap” for the
corresponding half-plane when discussing its geometry.
     The expected-depth algorithm uses probability mass in place of cardinality: S is mass-balanced
if some line has w(Sℓ− ), w(Sℓ+ ) ≥ δw(S), and mass-flat otherwise. In a mass-flat state, freezing the
mass-majority answer gives w(Cℓ ∩ S) < δw(S).
     Throughout the pseudocode, tables contain only realized hypotheses or patterns, ties use in-
put order, and a returned tree is contracted whenever a query has a constant answer. Cached
answers, the origin marker, and infeasible branches therefore create no additional supplied query.
The numerical parameters and their certificates are stated with the analysis of each routine.

Definition 2.5 (Alphabetic codes). For an ordered alphabet y1 < · · · < yr , a prefix code is
alphabetic if its codewords occur in that same lexicographic order, with 0 < 1. An ordered threshold
comparison asks whether the unknown outcome is at most yj for some j.

nodes are contracted, its two children separate that range at one threshold. Thus an alphabetic code
can be implemented by ordered threshold comparisons whenever those comparisons are available.
The code bits label the two ordered ranges; a line’s raw sign answer is routed to the matching range,
even if its sign encoding uses the opposite bit. This relabeling adds no query. An arbitrary prefix
code need not be executable by the supplied line family. When we call an identifying decision tree a
code, its internal queries must still be supplied lines. Section 3.4 verifies that the comparisons used
for its endpoint codes are available.

2.5    Convex geometry and finite set systems
Definition 2.13 (Planar convexity). A set P   is convex if it contains thePsegment joining any two of
its points. A convex combination is a sum j λj uj with λj ≥ 0 and j λj = 1. The convex hull
conv(U ) of a finite nonempty point set U is the set of its convex combinations. A vertex, or extreme
point, cannot be expressed as a convex combination of other points of the hull. The hull is the
convex hull of its vertices; set conv(∅) = ∅ by convention.
    A nonempty planar hull has affine dimension zero, one, or two according as it is a point, a
nontrivial segment, or has nonempty planar interior. In dimension two it is a convex polygon,
whose vertices have a cyclic boundary order; its edges join consecutive vertices. A supporting line
meets a convex set and leaves it entirely on one closed side. It exposes a point if that point is its
unique intersection with the set. A finite point set is in strictly convex position if every point is a
vertex of its hull.

    An open half-plane has the form {x : a · x > b} for a ̸= 0; replacing > by ≥ gives its closed
counterpart. A point is strictly on a chosen side when it is in that open half-plane, not on its
boundary. A common strict witness for several chosen sides is a point in all their open half-planes;
it need not itself be a hypothesis.

Theorem 2.14 (Planar Helly theorem). If every subfamily of at most three sets in a finite family
of planar convex sets has nonempty intersection, then the whole family has nonempty intersection.
The sets may be open.

   This is the standard planar form; see Matoušek [3].

Definition 2.15 (Covers and hitting sets). A finite set system consists of a universe U , a candidate
set V , and supplied subsets Fv ⊆ U indexed by v ∈ V . A cover of U is a choice of these sets whose
union contains every element of U . In the equivalent demand formulation, there is a candidate set
V and a subset Dh ⊆ V for each demand h. A hitting set is a subset P ⊆ V with P ∩ Dh ̸= ∅ for
every demand. Choosing v covers exactly Fv = {h : v ∈ Dh }. Thus covering demands by candidates
and hitting their subsets are the same incidence problem. A demand with Dh = ∅ is uncoverable.

    With positive demand weights, the weight covered by a choice is the sum of weights of demands
hit at least once; repeated hits are not counted again. An incidence table records the bits 1[h ∈ Fv ],
equivalently 1[v ∈ Dh ]. A cardinality budget is a nonnegative integer k permitting at most k
selected candidates. We may cap it at |V |, since there are no more distinct candidates to choose.

Definition 2.16 (Ordered and circular intervals). On a finite linear order v1 , . . . , vM , an ordinary
interval is a consecutive subset {vi , . . . , vj }; we also allow the empty interval. Its nonempty intervals
have first and last elements, called their endpoints. On a cyclic order, a circular interval is a
consecutive block with wrap-around allowed, also permitting the empty and whole-cycle sets. Two
vertices are adjacent when consecutive in this order, including vM , v1 on a cycle. An oriented
adjacent pair specifies which of those two vertices is first. A run of P in a linear candidate order is
a maximal nonempty consecutive block contained in P ; c(P ) counts such runs, with c(∅) = 0.

   Cutting a cyclic order at a removed vertex turns any circular interval avoiding that vertex into
an ordinary interval. When interval demands occur below, candidates are the ordered vertices, and
a demand is hit by selecting a vertex in its interval, not by selecting the interval itself.

3.1      A common majority witness
Lemma 3.1 (Common witness). In either kind of flat state, a point p strictly inside every majority
half-plane can be computed in polynomial time.

Proof. Use the measure defining flatness, either cardinality or probability. The union of any three
minority caps has measure less than 3δ times the measure of S, and therefore omits a hypothesis.
That hypothesis belongs strictly to all three majority half-planes, because no hypothesis lies on a
query line. The same argument covers subfamilies of fewer than three lines. Thus every subfamily
of at most three majority half-planes has nonempty intersection. Theorem 2.14 implies that the
whole family has nonempty intersection.
    To obtain a strict rational witness, orient each majority inequality as ai ·p−bi > 0 and maximize
t subject to ai · p − bi ≥ t for every i and 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. The open intersection supplies a feasible positive
t. This is a linear program: its objective and constraints are linear in the unknown coordinates of p
and the margin t. Rational linear programming therefore returns a strict witness with polynomial
encoding length [5].

3.4   Recovering the interval endpoints
After discovery, one positive hull vertex v is known. First inspect its cached neighbor answers.
If a neighbor is known to be negative, cut at that vertex and orient the order so that v is first.
The positive interval starts at v, so only its other endpoint needs a search. If both neighbors are
negative, the pattern consists of v alone and no endpoint query is needed.
    Otherwise use a geometric cut. If the origin lies on the hull boundary, cut at its free negative
marker. If it is interior, the ray from v through 0 meets an opposite hull edge with consecutive
endpoints a, b. The origin is a convex combination of v, a, b, with positive coefficient on v. Thus a, b
cannot both be positive: if all three dot products with xh exceeded one, their convex combination
would also exceed one, contradicting 0 · xh = 0. Cut the cyclic order between a and b. A circular
positive interval could wrap around this cut only if both endpoints were positive, which was just
excluded. If the ray meets a vertex, that vertex is necessarily negative and is itself a free cut point.
    No anchor query is made. The positive vertices now form an ordinary interval containing v. To
its left, querying a vertex asks whether the left endpoint is at or before that vertex. To its right,
querying a vertex asks whether the right endpoint is at or after it. Thus supplied hull queries give
exactly the ordered comparisons needed to locate the two endpoints.
    When neither endpoint is already fixed, learn the left endpoint, then the right endpoint con-
ditioned on the left endpoint and the earlier transcript. For consecutive possible left endpoints,
querying the earlier endpoint separates them; for consecutive possible right endpoints, querying the
later endpoint separates them. These are real hull vertices, so all comparisons used by the codes
are supplied queries. The conditional endpoint distributions come from the hypothesis table.
    If K has dimension at most one, it has at most two real vertices. With two real endpoints, one
positive endpoint forces the other negative because 0 lies between them. With one real vertex, its
positive bit is the complete nonzero pattern. Thus no further query is needed in either degenerate
case.
    A positive discovery exit is pinned if a neighbor of its discovered vertex has a cached negative
answer. It is singly pinned when exactly one neighbor is known negative, and doubly pinned when
both are. Pinning fixes one or both endpoints, as described above; it introduces no additional query.

4.3   Priority greedy with an enumerated adjacent pair
The comparison path gives a deadline for every pattern class, but the algorithm does not know that
path. It must produce one ordering that meets all deadlines at once. Enumerating the first adjacent
pair fixes the circular cut; priority weights then make ordinary maximum-remaining-weight greedy
behave like greedy for every deadline prefix, without requiring the comparison blocks as input.

lates a feasible block deadline into a bound on that actual first-positive position.

Lemma 4.5 (Adjacent-block schedule). Fix two distinct adjacent hull vertices v, w and consider
the pattern classes negative on both. Assign each such class an integer block budget dA ≥ 2. Suppose
a comparison sequence of support blocks starts with {v, w}, every other block is empty, a singleton,
or an adjacent pair, and it hits A in its first dA blocks.
    Force v, w along the negative discovery route. Give a class of budget d(j) the auxiliary integer
weight M r−j , where d(1) < · · · < d(r) are the distinct budgets and M = s + 1. Then complete
discovery by maximum-remaining-weight greedy. In this one ordering every surviving class satisfies

                                            gA ≤ 3dA − 2.

The schedule and its first-hit times are computable in polynomial time without knowing the compar-

Proof. Fix a budget d = d(j) and restrict demands to the prefix Pj = {A : dA ≤ d}. Every score
from these demands is an integer multiple of M r−j . The total weight of all later demands is less
than M r−j : there are at most s classes and each later weight is at most M r−j−1 , with s < M .
When there are no later demands this weight is zero.
     The weight of one prefix hit therefore outweighs every possible contribution from later-deadline
classes. Thus later demands cannot reverse a strict comparison of prefix scores. After the forced pair,
until the prefix is covered, each global greedy choice is a weighted greedy choice for its remaining
prefix demands. It hits at least one of them; a zero prefix score cannot beat a positive prefix score.
Ties may use later weights but are legitimate greedy ties for the prefix.
     Cut at v and delete w. The remaining demands are ordinary intervals. Because the removed
vertices were adjacent, every comparison pair avoiding them is still consecutive in this linear can-
didate order; a block meeting them becomes at most a singleton. Let P be the union of the first d
comparison blocks with v, w removed, and let c(P ) count its runs of consecutive candidates. The
first block contributes nothing. Each later block contributes at most three to size plus run count: an
adjacent pair has size two and one run, while a singleton has size one and one run. Both quantities
are subadditive under union, so
                                        |P | + c(P ) ≤ 3(d − 1).
This set covers the surviving prefix. For any nonempty surviving prefix, its classes are negative on
v, w and therefore must be hit in a later comparison block, so P is nonempty. If it is nonempty,

the forced pair gives 3d − 2. The priority argument applies to every prefix in the same ordering.

5     Scheduling the first positive answer
The maximum-depth algorithm schedules discovery around the heights of its child codes. For
expected depth, a cap containing a likely target should instead be encountered early. This is a
weighted min-sum set cover (MSSC) problem. The sets remain the supplied caps; singleton sets are
not added unless an actual supplied test realizes them.
   In the decision-tree application, a positive hull answer exits the all-negative route and reveals
that the target lies in a small cap. The all-zero pattern has no positive answer, so on the full-cover
route it is identified after every positive pattern has been covered (and an earlier singleton inference
only shortens that route). Its contribution is therefore at most its weight times the schedule length.
This is why the ordering analysis keeps both the first-cover sum and a separate length term.

6.6   Implementation and running time
Each stage computes a planar linear program, a convex hull, its explicit pattern table, a minimum
circular-interval cover and the budgeted-coverage dynamic programs of Lemma 5.1. There are at
most |V | distinct budgets and polynomially many shift intervals. The two-support representation
and the free opposite-edge cut are computable by planar convex-hull operations. Endpoint codes
use polynomial interval dynamic programs. Discovery’s positive exits and terminal singleton are
disjoint subsets of S, so there are at most |S| nonempty transcripts. Each conditioned endpoint
problem has at most |S| possible values. Thus the number and size of the dynamic programs are
polynomial.
    Each nonterminal stage partitions its state into at least two nonempty proper children, so the
recursive stage hierarchy has at most n − 1 internal stages. Constant questions, cached questions,
and empty branches are suppressed. The actual complete binary decision tree then has n−1 internal
nodes. All arithmetic and comparisons have polynomial bit complexity. In particular, the algorithm
compares rational masses and optimizes rational dynamic-program values; logarithms appear only
in the analysis. The fixed balance threshold is exactly 1234989569/1010 , and β = 3512863/106 is
rational as well.
    The proof uses no lower bound on hypothesis probabilities. A rare pattern may require many
queries, but its small mass also reduces its contribution to both accounting sums. This is why the
argument controls expected depth rather than maximum depth.


7     A polynomial improvement by scheduling adjacent blocks
The individual-vertex schedule of Section 6 can be improved without increasing the running time
to quasipolynomial. Apply the same exact-coverage and geometric-batch algorithm to adjacent
hull edges, and account for discovery and endpoint decoding together. This first gives a factor

proof has one ledger: discovery gives an optimal-path restriction credit, endpoint recovery leaves a

12     Polynomial maximum depth with rank-subset scheduling
The direct maximum-depth guarantee in Section 11 can be obtained in polynomial time. The key is
an exact interval-deadline algorithm exponential in the total number of schedule slots, rather than
in the number of distinct deadline grades. Restrict this algorithm to a logarithmic number of slots
and retain the priority schedule as a fallback. Two simultaneous recursive bounds then cover all
instances. The algorithm checks schedules against the heights of its own stored children. An optimal
witness path is used only to prove that a passing schedule exists; neither that path nor its optimum
height is an input.

candidate in that interval must occur by rank d[a,b] . Selected candidates are distinct, and the total
schedule length is at most L.

16     Strong NP-completeness
The approximation algorithms exploit planar structure, but exact optimization remains hard. We
first state the decision and source problems and the graph terminology used by both reductions.

Definition 16.1 (Complexity and reductions). A polynomial-time many-one reduction maps in-
stances of one decision problem to another in polynomial time, so an input is a yes-instance if and
only if its image is a yes-instance.
     A decision problem is in NP if every yes-instance has a certificate of polynomial encoding length
whose validity is checkable in polynomial time, and no certificate is accepted on a no-instance. It is
NP-hard if every problem in NP admits such a reduction to it, and NP-complete if it is both NP-
hard and in NP. A numerical problem is strongly NP-hard if hardness persists when its integer data
are bounded by a fixed polynomial in the combinatorial instance size; it is strongly NP-complete if
it is also in NP. For rational data, our restricted instances explicitly bound both numerators and
denominators.

refers to the number of edges, not their weights. A perfect matching leaves no vertex unmatched.

and supply singleton and adjacent-pair membership tests. A maximum matching leaves one even-
position vertex unmatched; its position chooses the item’s bin. Moving that position changes a short
part of a histogram. The maximum-depth reduction counts branch heights, while the expected-depth
reduction counts pair weights. In both cases these local changes add to the source bin loads.

17     Uniform expected-depth hardness
The weighted reduction of Section 16 also yields uniform-prior hardness, but a direct replacement
of a weight by ordinary points requires a normalization argument. We prove an exact replacement
theorem. It preserves the weighted optimum up to a computable additive term, despite the addi-
tional private queries. Throughout this section, raw uniform cost means the sum of the leaf depths,
before division by the number of hypotheses.

17.1    A rotation for a private singleton
Lemma 17.1 (Uniform singleton rotation). Suppose a proper identifying tree on a uniformly
weighted state S first asks a supplied query isolating x ∈ S, and the other child first asks a query r
that is proper on S \ {x}. These two queries can be exchanged, placing the singleton query in the
answer branch of r containing x, without increasing the raw cost.



                                                  91
Proof. Let A, B be the two nonempty answer classes of r on S \ {x}, and choose their names so that

attach the original subtrees on A and B. The depth of x increases by one, the depth of every point
of B decreases by one, and all other depths are unchanged. The change in raw cost is 1 − |B| ≤ 0.
The resulting queries are proper and are members of the original supplied family.

17.3    Uniform-prior strong NP-completeness
Theorem 17.4. Deciding whether the optimal expected depth is at most a rational threshold is
strongly NP-complete under a uniform prior, even for integer-coordinate points on a parabola, poly-

Proof. Apply Algorithm 22 to the hard weighted linear forest instances of Theorem 16.8. If the
original number of hypotheses is n0 = N + 1, that construction P    has nonzero weights O(n30 ) and

    The common-witness normalization is the structural step shared by both algorithms. In a flat
state, it turns each supplied test into an opposite cap and represents that cap by at most two
adjacent vertices of a convex hull. For each hypothesis, the positive hull answers form a circular
interval. This representation does not enlarge the query family: it supplies an interval view of the
tests that were already given.
    The two objectives then use that structure differently. For maximum depth, the algorithm
schedules discovery around the actual heights of its recursively built children. The comparison path
in an optimal tree supplies the deadline credits, while adjacent support blocks control discovery
delay; route-specific endpoint searches preserve those credits. Exact Cartesian-tree scheduling with
rank subsets handles logarithmically many discovery slots in polynomial time. A global slot cap
and the priority fallback support simultaneous small-optimum and universal bounds, yielding factor
4.464966. For expected depth, the algorithm learns each full hull pattern before recursing. Queries
that become unnecessary after restriction pay for discovery, and the entropy still remaining after
discovery pays for endpoint information. Exact integer-budget coverage applied to adjacent hull
blocks, joint discovery and endpoint accounting, and the zero-pattern waiting time sharpen the
polynomial factor. Retaining an orientation credit through the first two batches’ coverage guarantees
lowers it to 5.283310. Rational choices between stopping and querying both endpoints retain the
second answer’s conditional information and lower it further to 5.272565. Coarsening the transcript
preserves the individual cap bound without charging the information twice. Changing the initial
coverage budget and merging selected first phases lowers the factor to 5.197569. Modifying both
initial budgets lowers it further to 5.182976. Signed first-cover corrections and a separate all-negative
waiting calculation account for every effect of these changed schedules. In both analyses, the local
credits are decreases of quantities that telescope over the recursive tree.
    The quasipolynomial algorithms retain a logarithmic number of schedule grades, giving direct
factors below 4.464966 and 3.7187. Their additive bounds have leading coefficient arbitrarily close
to two. A logarithmic-depth lookahead dynamic program then removes the count and entropy
surcharge from the ratio, proving factors 2 + ε for every fixed ε > 0. The on-line-answer entries
follow from the same factor-two simulation, using the binary guarantee with parameter ε/2. These
results have quasipolynomial running time.
    The small-cap results place additional limits on potential hardness constructions. With a dis-
tinguished all-miss hypothesis and fixed cap size, maximum depth is exactly solvable in polynomial
time. The weighted singleton/pair path-and-cycle subclass with all singleton tests has an EPTAS
for expected depth. Thus the current weighted pair-path reduction cannot yield APX-hardness
unless P = NP. Uniform fixed-size caps in convex position with an all-miss hypothesis also admit
a PTAS, without requiring singleton tests. Clearing short gaps permits exact optimization of the
residual block interleavings; the prefix cost is a small fraction of the full optimum. Constant-factor
hardness must therefore escape this additional restricted class.
    The hardness reductions show that this geometry does not make exact optimization easy: even

imability remains open. Whether the polynomial factors can be lowered or the quasipolynomial
factors can be brought below two remains open.
